Emission and absorption spectra

Every element gives out, and takes in, its own exact set of colours. Start with what a spectroscope shows, go inside one atom to see why, then use the patterns to work out which elements are in an unknown sample.

A hot solid, such as a lamp filament, gives out every wavelength. The screen shows an unbroken band from violet to red. Choose Hot filament to see it.

A hot, low-pressure gas glows with only a few exact wavelengths. The screen is dark apart from separate bright lines. Change the gas and the lines change.

Electrons in shells

Electrons orbit the nucleus in shells at fixed distances. An electron in a shell further from the nucleus has more energy. It cannot sit in between two shells.

Giving out radiation

When an electron falls to a lower shell, the atom emits electromagnetic radiation. Each possible fall gives out one exact wavelength. A bigger fall gives out more energy, which means a shorter wavelength.

Absorbing radiation

An atom can absorb electromagnetic radiation, and an electron moves to a higher shell. It only works if the radiation carries exactly the energy for that jump. Every other wavelength passes straight through.

Why lines, not a rainbow

The shells are fixed, so only certain jumps are possible, and each jump is one line at one wavelength. Different elements have different shells, so each has its own set of lines.

Dark lines match bright lines

Absorbing and emitting use the same jumps between the same shells. So the dark lines in an element's absorption spectrum sit at the same wavelengths as the bright lines in its emission spectrum.

Where the absorbed light goes

The electron soon falls back and the atom gives the light out again, but in a random direction. Very little of it carries on towards the screen, so the line still looks dark.

Energy levels

An electron in an atom can only have certain energies. Each allowed energy is a shell, or energy level. The electron cannot sit in between, so it can only gain or lose energy in fixed amounts.

One jump, one photon

When an electron falls from a higher level to a lower one, the energy it loses leaves as a single photon. The photon's energy sets its frequency:

A bigger fall means a higher frequency and a shorter wavelength.

Why lines, not a rainbow

The gaps between levels are fixed, so only certain photon energies are possible. Each one is a line at one wavelength:

Different elements have different levels, so each has its own set of lines.

Absorption uses the same gaps

An atom can only absorb a photon whose energy exactly matches a gap between two of its levels. Those photons are removed from white light, and all the others pass through. The dark lines sit at the same wavelengths as the bright ones.

Where the absorbed light goes

The excited atom soon gives the photon out again, but in a random direction. Very little of it carries on in the direction of the original beam, so the line still looks dark against the bright spectrum either side.

A fingerprint

No two elements have the same set of lines. If every bright line of an element appears in a spectrum, at exactly the right wavelengths, that element is present.

Mixtures

A mixture shows the lines of all its elements at once. To decipher it, match one element at a time and tick off its lines, until every line is explained.

Position, not colour

Two elements can both have a yellow line. What matters is the exact wavelength, and whether the element's other lines are there too. One matching line is never enough.

What it is used for

Spectra tell astronomers what stars are made of without going there. The same idea identifies the metal in a flame test and checks the gas in a discharge lamp.
