The simple motor and loudspeaker

One coil of wire, two magnets, and a split ring that swaps the connections every half turn. Run it, turn it by hand, then reverse the cell or swap the magnets and predict which way it will go. The loudspeaker at the end uses the same force to make sound.

Exam-style explanation

The motor effect on a single wire

  1. There is a current in the wire.
  2. This creates a magnetic field around the wire.
  3. This interacts with the permanent magnetic field.
  4. This causes a resultant force on the wire, so the wire accelerates.
  5. You can find the direction of the force using Fleming's left-hand rule.

Exam-style explanation

The motor effect in a motor

  1. There is a current in the coil.
  2. This creates a magnetic field around the coil.
  3. This interacts with the permanent magnetic field.
  4. This causes a resultant force on the coil.
  5. The direction of the force is opposite on either side of the coil.
  6. So the coil rotates.

The motor effect

A wire carrying a current across a magnetic field feels a force at right angles to both the current and the field:

Only the two long sides of the coil count. The field runs across them, so one is pushed up and the other down.

Fleming's left-hand rule

First finger along the field (N to S), second finger along the current, and the thumb gives the force. Pause the motor, pick a side of the coil, and check the green arrow against your hand.

The split ring

Each half of the ring is joined to one side of the coil and turns with it. The brushes stay still. Every half turn the two halves swap brushes, so the current in the coil reverses and the side that is now on the left is still pushed the same way.

Why it needs the split ring

Without it, the yellow side would be pushed up on one side of the axle and still pushed up on the other, which would turn the coil back again. It would rock to the upright position and stop.

The force stays the same

While current flows, the long sides are always at right angles to the field, so the force on each is the same size. What changes is how far the force acts from the axle:

When the coil is flat the distance is greatest. As it turns towards upright the distance shrinks, so the turning effect falls.

The dead zone

Near upright, each brush is over a gap in the split ring. The circuit is broken, no current flows, and the force arrows disappear. A running motor coasts through on its own momentum, then the brushes meet the other halves of the ring and the forces return. Stop it in the dead zone and it will not start again without a push.

Reversing it

Reverse the cell and the current in each side reverses, so both forces reverse. Swap the magnets and the field reverses, with the same result. Do both and the motor turns the original way.

Making it better

More turns on the coil, a larger current or stronger magnets all give a larger force. Real motors also use several coils at different angles, so there is never a dead spot. The hairdryer motor shows how.

Also the motor effectThe loudspeaker

Exam-style explanation

The motor effect in a loudspeaker

  1. There is a current in the coil.
  2. This creates a magnetic field around the coil.
  3. This interacts with the permanent magnetic field.
  4. This causes a resultant force on the coil.
  5. The direction of the force is the same on either side of the coil.
  6. So the coil moves forwards or backwards. You can find the direction using Fleming's left-hand rule.
  7. With an alternating current, the force alternates back and forth at the same frequency as the current.

The same force, in a straight line

The coil sits in the gap between the centre pole and the outer ring, where the field points outwards. The current runs round the coil, at right angles to that field, so the force on every part of the coil is along the axis:

Use Fleming's left-hand rule on any circle in Fig 5 to check the arrow.

Why it vibrates

The signal is an alternating current. Each time the current reverses, the force reverses, so the coil is pushed out, then pulled in. The cone is fixed to the coil and moves with it.

Making sound

The moving cone pushes the air in front of it, squashing it and then stretching it. Those pressure changes travel away as a sound wave with the same frequency as the current.

Pitch and volume

A higher frequency current makes the cone vibrate more times each second: a higher note. A larger current gives a larger force, so the cone moves further: a louder sound. Try the Pitch and Volume buttons.

A steady current

Pause it and drag Current by hand. The cone moves to a new position and stays there. A steady current makes no sound, because the cone has to keep moving to make a wave.

Why the magnet is this shape

With a centre pole inside a ring, the field crosses the gap in every direction outwards, so the whole length of the coil sits in the field and every part of it is pushed the same way.
